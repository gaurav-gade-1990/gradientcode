## 1. What the problem is really asking

On the surface this is a hiring plan: how many people, at what levels, by when. Underneath, it's a pay-fairness question with a fixed budget and a seasonal deadline. The VP Product's mix of 2 staff, 6 senior and 4 mid-level engineers fails one test or the other. At the company's bands it fits the money, but offers get turned down and the team arrives too late for the back-to-school release. At market rates it gets accepted but blows the $3.6M cap. And the obvious escape, paying new hires more, is exactly what the comp committee has ruled out.

Three facts in the brief carry the case:

1. **The level mix is the lever.** The cap won't move and the pay rule is fixed, so the only thing you control is how many people you hire at each level. That's a leadership call: what does a brand-new team actually need on day one?
2. **The calendar has a price.** The AI tutor is worth $6M of first-year ARR if it ships for back-to-school, so every month late is about $500k. Cheap but slow offers aren't cheap.
3. **Fairness is judged by people who aren't in the plan.** The 40 L5 engineers in product engineering will compare their pay with the new AI L5s. A range they can reach through the same loop is defensible; a premium for strangers isn't.

The interviewer is grading whether you can make a cost, calendar and fairness trade at the same time, and whether you can tell the VP Product clearly what they're giving up.

## 2. The numbers that drive the decision

Work these out first. They're the whole economics of the case.

**The VP Product's mix.**
- At band: 2 × $370k + 6 × $290k + 4 × $230k = **$3.40M**.
- At market: 2 × $460k + 6 × $350k + 4 × $260k = **$4.06M**, which is **$460k** over the cap.

**Acceptance and the calendar.**
- At band: 3 of 11 offers accepted = **27%**. 5 of the 8 who declined cited an offer one level higher, which is leveling pressure, not just pay.
- At market: about **70%** (the brief's engineering figure; treat it as an assumption to watch).
- The recruiter makes about 4 offers a month. First offers go out 1.5 months after roles open, and hires start a month after accepting.
- Band plan, 8 external hires: 8 / 27% = **29 offers**, 29 / 4 ≈ 7.3 months of offers, so the last start is 1.5 + 7.3 + 1 ≈ **month 10**.
- Recommended plan, 7 external hires: 7 / 70% = **10 offers**, 10 / 4 = 2.5 months, so the last start is 1.5 + 2.5 + 1 = **month 5**.

**The price of being late.** $6M / 12 = **$500k** a month. The band plan is about 3.8 months late, so about **$1.9M** of first-year ARR is deferred (assuming the release waits for the team).

**Mixes that fit at market.**
- 1 L6 / 5 L5 / 5 L4 (11 heads): $460k + $1.75M + $1.30M = **$3.51M**, $90k under.
- 1 L6 / 3 L5 / 8 L4 (12 heads): $460k + $1.05M + $2.08M = **$3.59M**, $10k under.
- The 12th head as an L4 on top of the 11 would cost $260k; with the $90k headroom, the ask is **$170k**.

**Fairness.** Extending the AI range to all 40 L5 engineers: 40 × $60k = **$2.4M a year**. That's why the range has to be tied to the role.

**Triage.** 24 engineer-months: 4 engineers take 6 months, 3 take **8**.

Forty-five minutes. The options table and the economics carry the most marks.

## 4. Pillar A: Frame

### Situation

**The question it answers:** what exactly is being decided, by when, and which numbers frame it?

**How to reason.** Rewrite "hire 12 AI engineers" as "a team in seats before the month-6 back-to-school release, inside $3.6M, under one fair pay rule". That gives you three tests every option must pass. Then show the VP Product's mix failing them: $3.40M at band fits but fills too slowly, and $4.06M at market is over. Add the acceptance data (27%, with declines citing higher levels) and the revenue clock ($500k a month). Say what's out of scope: product design and pay outside the AI role.

**The trap.** Accepting the requested mix as fixed. A new team needs one architect, not two, and the mix is where the money is.

**Rubric criteria earned:** the decision restated with the date, cap and pay rule; the mix at market and at band; the acceptance rate and its calendar effect; something out of scope.

### Stakeholders

**The question it answers:** who has a stake, what does each want, and how do you bring them along?

**How to reason.** This is a Medium case because the stakeholders pull in different directions. The CFO holds the cap. HR and the comp committee hold the pay rule. The VP Product wants 12 people and two staff engineers. Recruiting wants offers that close. Engineering managers lose 4 transfers and need backfills. The 40 L5 engineers outside AI are the quiet jury: they decide whether the plan feels fair.

Name the VP Product's loss explicitly: a staff engineer, a head, and two months on triage. Offer what you can: the tutor team fully staffed, and a dated revisit of the 12th head.

**The trap.** Leaving the 40 L5 engineers out of the table. They're the people the comp committee is protecting.

**Rubric criteria earned:** CFO, HR, VP Product and recruiting each with a distinct want; the VP Product's give-up; the 40 L5s and the managers; one idea to hold.

## 5. Pillar B: Decide

### Options

**The question it answers:** what are the real hiring and pay options?

**How to reason.** Put five options on one table: hire at band (the do-nothing on pay), a premium for new hires only, the AI range with the VP's mix, the AI range with a 1 / 5 / 5 mix, and the AI range with a 1 / 3 / 8 mix. Add rows for external hires, offers needed and the last start month, because those turn acceptance rates into a calendar. The premium for new hires is ruled out by the comp committee. Hiring at band fills around month 10. The VP's mix at market is $460k over. The 1 / 3 / 8 mix fits but leaves one staff and three seniors leading eight mid-level engineers.

**The trap.** Solving the budget by leveling people lower than the loop says. That's the new-hire premium in disguise, and the inequity runs the other way.

**Rubric criteria earned:** bands as the do-nothing; the new-hire premium ruled out with reasons; the band calendar to month 10; one comparable table.

### Decision and Trade-offs

**The question it answers:** what do you recommend, what do you give up, and what would change it?

**How to reason.** Recommend the AI range with 11 hires at 1 / 5 / 5, for $3.51M: the 4 transfers plus 7 external hires. Then list the give-ups: the 12th head, the second staff engineer, triage on 3 instead of 4 (8 months instead of 6), and a visible pay gap with non-AI L5s that's justified by role and open through the loop.

Reversibility splits in two. The mix is reversible, since the 12th seat can still be filled. The range isn't, in practice, because nobody cuts pay, so it gets a yearly review instead. Give the month-6 triggers for the 12th seat: the tutor live with a paying pilot, triage more than 2 months behind, and acceptance at or above 60%.

**The trap.** Promising all 12 to keep the VP Product happy. Being plain about the trade is what earns the later ask.

**Rubric criteria earned:** one recommendation inside the cap; the 12th head and second staff engineer named; reversibility of mix and range; dated triggers.

### Economics

**The question it answers:** what does each option cost over three years, and what does a late team cost?

**How to reason.** Multiply each run-rate by 3: $10.20M at band, $12.18M for the VP's mix at market, $10.53M recommended and $10.77M bottom-heavy. Then add the cost the band plan hides: about $1.9M of deferred ARR from a team that's 3.8 months late. The recommended plan costs $330k more than the band plan over 3 years and avoids that.

Two more numbers show level. Extending the range to all 40 L5s would cost $2.4M a year, which is why it's scoped to the role. And the transfers' backfills come out of engineering's own plan, so be honest that the company's total cost is higher than the AI budget shows. Label every pay figure as assumed.

**The trap.** Calling band offers "cheaper". At 27% acceptance they're the most expensive plan once the calendar is priced.

**Rubric criteria earned:** 3-year run cost per option; late-team cost at $500k a month; the $2.4M all-L5 figure; labelled comp and backfill ownership.

## 6. Pillar C: Execute

### Org and Ownership

**The question it answers:** who owns leveling, offers and the range?

**How to reason.** Separate the people who decide the level (a panel with two AI leads and one engineering lead from outside AI) from the person who negotiates (the recruiter, within the range for that level). HR owns the range, its written criteria and its yearly review. Then allocate the 11: AI tutor 5, shared evaluation and platform 3 led by the L6, triage 3.

**The trap.** Letting the hiring manager level and close. Under deadline pressure, levels drift up to win offers, and the 5 declines citing a higher level tell you that pressure is real.

**Rubric criteria earned:** level decided apart from the offer; the 11 allocated; HR owning the range.

### Plan and Milestones

**The question it answers:** how do you get people in seats before the release?

**How to reason.** Transfers start in month 1, so the tutor work begins with people who already know the codebase and the customers. Open the staff engineer search first, because it's the hardest seat and it sets the architecture. Run 10 offers at 4 a month from month 1.5 to month 4, with a gate after the first 5 offers: if acceptance is below 60%, switch to the fallback. The last start is month 5, the back-to-school release at month 6, and the 12th-head revisit happens at the same time.

**The trap.** Opening all seven roles at once with one recruiter. The staff search starves while mid-level offers queue.

**Rubric criteria earned:** transfers in month 1 and staff first; offers and calendar shown; a measurable gate; the month-6 revisit.

### Risk and Governance

**The question it answers:** what could go wrong with fairness, leveling and process?

**How to reason.** Fairness is a process you can audit. Use the same structured loop and scoring for internal and external candidates, and compare pass rates. Write the AI role criteria down so the range has a documented, job-based reason. Allow flexibility within the range or on start date, never across a level. Keep the transfer track open on a published schedule. If offers are still declined at market, fall back to the 1 / 3 / 8 mix or a sign-on bonus inside the $90k headroom.

**The trap.** Treating "market rate" as a justification on its own. Without written criteria, two L5s on different pay is hard to defend.

**Rubric criteria earned:** same loop for both; written criteria; no up-leveling; a fallback.

## 7. Pillar D: Land

### Metrics

**The question it answers:** how do you know the plan worked, and what would show harm?

**How to reason.** Pair each success metric with a guard. Acceptance (target 70%) is guarded by the share of hires leveled above the panel's call, which should be zero. Seats filled by month 5 are guarded by first-year regretted attrition. Run-rate under $3.6M is guarded by sign-on spend staying inside the headroom. The transfer track is guarded by attrition among L5 engineers outside AI, which is where getting fairness wrong would show.

**The trap.** Measuring time to fill alone. A fast fill that inflates levels or pushes out the 40 is a slow failure.

**Rubric criteria earned:** paired guards; acceptance against 70%; non-AI L5 attrition; run-rate against the cap.

### Communication

**The question it answers:** what does each audience hear, and how often?

**How to reason.** One line per audience, built from your numbers: exec staff hear 11 by month 5 for $3.51M; the CFO hears $90k under and a $170k ask only on results; the VP Product hears exactly what they give up; the comp committee hears one range per level, transfers included; the engineering org hears the path into the range. Timing matters: tell engineering about the range and the transfer window before external offers go out. Weekly status to product, monthly to exec staff.

**The trap.** Keeping the range quiet. Pay leaks within weeks, and then the story is told for you.

**Rubric criteria earned:** five or more audiences; the path into the range announced first; the VP Product's give-up said plainly; a cadence.

## 8. The decisions that separate strong answers

**Using the mix, not the pay rule, as the lever.** Weak answers try to bend pay: a new-hire premium, or lower levels. Strong answers accept the rule and change the shape of the team, then defend the shape.

**Pricing the calendar.** Band offers look $110k cheaper a year than the recommended plan. Once 27% acceptance becomes a team that's complete around month 10, they cost about $1.9M of deferred ARR.

**Making fairness reachable.** A range tied to a role, with a published loop and transfer window, turns a pay gap into an opportunity.

## 9. Common mistakes

- **Hiring the requested mix and asking for more money.** The CFO said the cap won't move.
- **Up-leveling to close.** It wins one offer and breaks the ladder for everyone after.
- **Forgetting the 40 L5s.** They're who the comp committee is protecting.
- **Assuming transfers are free.** Someone backfills them.
- **No fallback.** The 70% acceptance at market is an assumption, so have a plan if it's wrong.
- **Tool talk.** Listing the frameworks the hires should know instead of levels, cost and the calendar.

## 10. Follow-ups an interviewer might ask

**A strong L5 candidate has an L6 offer elsewhere. Do you match the level?**
No. The panel's level stands. You can move to the top of the L5 AI range or offer a sign-on from the headroom, and you can say when the L6 promotion case would be reviewed. One up-level becomes the reference point for every later offer.

**An L5 engineer outside AI says the range is unfair.**
Agree that the gap is real, and show the written role criteria and the next transfer window. If they pass the loop, they move into the range on the same terms as everyone else.

**The CFO cuts the budget to $3.4M.**
At market, 1 / 5 / 5 doesn't fit. The options are fewer heads (for example 1 / 5 / 4 at $3.25M) or fewer seniors. Protect the tutor team's 5 and cut triage first, since it has no revenue attached.

**What if acceptance at market is only 50%?**
7 / 50% = 14 offers, 3.5 months of offers, so the last start slips to month 6. Use the gate after the first 5 offers to switch early: move one L5 seat to an L4 or use sign-on bonuses.

**Why not use contractors for the release?**
They can add capacity, but the release needs people who will own the product after back-to-school. Use them for bounded work, such as evaluation labeling, and not for the core team.

### Context

You are the Group Chief AI Officer at a European life and health insurer: €11.2B of gross written premium, 9 country businesses, 26,000 employees. You joined three months ago. Your first census found 212 AI systems in production; central IT knew about 140 of them. Last week the lead insurance supervisor wrote to the board: in 6 months it will run a thematic review of AI governance and will ask for the inventory, one named accountable executive, the risk assessment for every system the group treats as high-risk, and evidence that the board oversees AI risk. The CEO wants your proposed operating model at the board risk committee in 3 weeks.

### What you have

- **Inventory.** 212 systems. Legal's preliminary tiering: 14 **high-risk** (life and health underwriting and pricing models, and HR candidate screening, which legal reads as high-risk under the EU AI Act); 61 **material** under the group's model risk policy (customer-facing, or affecting claims or reserves); 137 **low** (internal drafting and productivity, always with human review). Of the 72 systems central IT didn't know about, 51 belong to the 3 countries that run their own data science teams.
- **Governance today.** 4 people in group compliance review every proposal centrally, each with the same full assessment. 46 proposals are queued (3 high-risk, 17 material, 26 low) and the median wait is 11 weeks. New proposals arrive at about 8 a month: on recent history 0.5 high-risk, 2.5 material and 5 low.
- **Effort per assessment** (the team's average over its last 20 reviews, and its estimates for the lighter forms): full assessment 120 hours; light assessment 24 hours; checking a self-attestation 3 hours.
- **Capacity.** A reviewer has about 130 productive review hours a month (assumed).
- **People.** The CFO has frozen group headcount but will approve up to 4 new central governance hires at €160k a year fully loaded (assumed); hiring takes 3 months. Each of the 9 countries has an operational-risk team, and its CEO could release 0.5 of a person as a country AI risk lead from month 2 (€130k a year fully loaded, assumed), but only if they agree to.
- **Budget.** €1.5M of new spend in year one for governance: hires, tooling and outside assurance. An inventory and workflow tool is quoted at €250k a year. An external assurance review of a high-risk system is quoted at €35k each.
- **Value at stake.** The claims automation programme is worth €38M a year in savings at full run rate, and 9 of the 17 queued material proposals belong to it. The new instant-issue term life product carries €210M of new annual premium in the plan and depends on one high-risk underwriting model.
- **Policy.** The board's risk appetite statement allows no material supervisory finding. The group has no AI policy yet; it has a model risk policy written for actuarial and pricing models.

### The constraint

The supervisor's date is fixed, and the country CEOs own their P&Ls. The 3 countries with their own data science teams have told the CEO they will not route every AI change through a group queue that already takes 11 weeks. Central control is what the supervisor will look for; country ownership is what keeps the 72 unknown systems from becoming 150. You can't have both in full, and you can't staff a central team big enough to review everything by month 6.

### What a strong answer covers

- How much assessment work the supervisor's date really requires, and whether any central team you can hire can do it.
- Who is accountable for AI risk, who approves, and how the people who build systems stay out of approving them.
- How you get the three data science countries to report what they build instead of hiding it.
- What it costs over three years, and what the 11-week queue already costs.
- What the board, the supervisor and the country CEOs each hear, and what would make you change the model.

## 1. What the problem is really asking

On the surface this is a budget variance: $24.6M against $18M. Underneath, it's a trust question from a board. The audit committee chair isn't asking you to justify AI. They're asking two things a former CFO always asks: does management control this spend, and is the number we gave investors still safe?

Three facts in the brief carry the case, and each is easy to skim past:

1. **The year can't come back to plan.** $17.7M was already spent by the end of Q3, leaving $0.3M of the $18M. Any answer that offers "we'll get back to budget" hasn't done the subtraction.
2. **The biggest overrun is a success.** The service agent is over because it resolved 1.5M contacts instead of 1.0M, and every one of those avoided a $7.00 contact-center fee. Budgeting a variable cost as a fixed sum turned good news into a variance.
3. **The plan already counted some savings.** Guidance was built on the operating plan, which assumed $7.0M from the agent and $3.0M from content. Savings above that offset the overrun, so the gross 27.5 bp and the net effect on margin are very different numbers.

The interviewer is grading whether you can run the room at board level: name the bad number first, separate the causes, take decisions before you ask for anything, and answer the hard questions without spin.

## 2. The numbers that drive the decision

Work these out in the first eight minutes. Every later section reuses them.

**The overrun.**
- Plan $18.0M; forecast 6.2 + 2.4 + 6.8 + 5.6 + 3.6 = **$24.6M**; over by **$6.6M**, or 6.6 / 18 = **36.7%**.
- Against revenue: $6.6M / $2.4B = **27.5 basis points**. The cushion is 10 bp.

**What's left to change.**
- Q4 forecast: 2.0 + 0.5 + 1.9 + 1.6 + 0.9 = **$6.9M**. Spent so far: 24.6 − 6.9 = **$17.7M**, which is 98% of plan.
- Getting to $18M would mean cutting $6.6M of the $6.9M left, including the agent's $2.0M during the holiday peak.

**Splitting the overrun by cause.**
- Agent +$2.2M. At the planned $4.00 a contact ($4.0M / 1.0M), 0.5M extra contacts cost $2.0M, so $2.0M is volume and $0.2M is unit-cost creep. That's success.
- Forecasting +$1.8M from the pipeline rebuild: slippage.
- Marketing +$2.1M with no measurable lift, plus copilot 1,000 extra seats × $50 × 12 = $0.6M: waste, $2.7M together.
- Content −$0.1M.

**Savings above plan.**
- Agent: (1.5M − 1.0M) × $7.00 = **$3.5M**. Content: $3.1M − $3.0M = **$0.1M**. Total **$3.6M**.
- Net hit with no action: 6.6 − 3.6 = **$3.0M = 12.5 bp**, above the cushion.

**The Q4 actions.**
- Pause marketing: saves its **$1.6M** Q4 spend.
- Cut seats 6,000 → 2,500: 3,500 × $50 × 3 = **$0.525M**.
- Year-end: 24.6 − 1.6 − 0.525 = **$22.475M**, $4.475M over (24.9%, 18.6 bp gross).
- Net: 4.475 − 3.6 = **$0.875M = 3.6 bp**, inside the cushion.

**Value per initiative.**
- Agent: 1.5M × $7.00 = $10.5M; per contact $6.2M / 1.5M = **$4.13**, netting $2.87.
- Marketing: $800M × 0.3% × 42% = **$1.0M** gross profit; the ±0.6 point range runs from −0.3% to +0.9%, so −$1.0M to +$3.0M.
- Forecasting: $4.5M × 20% (assumed carrying cost) = **$0.9M a year**. If each region repeats the pilot (assumed), 9 regions give $4.5M × 9/2 = **$20.25M**, half of the $40M promised.
- Copilot: 6,000 × 31% = **1,860** weekly users; $3.6M / 1,860 = $1,935 a year per active user.
- Total measured value: 10.5 + 3.1 + 0.9 + 1.0 = **$15.5M**.

**All-in.** Team 30 × $210k (assumed) = **$6.3M**, so $22.5M + $6.3M = **$28.8M** against $15.5M.

## 3. How to spend your time

Sixty minutes, about twelve per pillar, with time at each end for the numbers and a final check.

| Minutes | What you do |
|---|---|
| 0–8 | Read the brief. Write down $6.6M, 27.5 bp, $17.7M spent, $3.6M extra savings, 12.5 bp net. Circle "10 basis points of cushion" and "billed monthly". |
| 8–18 | **Pillar A.** The real question (control and guidance), the cause split, and the stakeholders you pre-brief. |
| 18–36 | **Pillar B.** Four framings on one table, the Q4 actions, and the economics per initiative and net against plan. This pillar carries the most marks. |
| 36–46 | **Pillar C.** Owners of value, the nine-day plan and the gates, the risk of a second surprise. |
| 46–57 | **Pillar D.** Metrics with guards, then the opening line, the slide and the two hard answers. Say them out loud. |
| 57–60 | Check every number is derived or labelled. |

## 4. Pillar A: Frame

### Situation

**The question it answers:** what is the board really asking, and which numbers frame the answer?

**How to reason.** Translate the audit chair's "why are we over?" into the two questions behind it: is spend controlled, and is guidance safe? Then build a small frame table: the overrun, its effect in basis points against the cushion, how much is already spent, the savings above plan, and the team cost outside the budget. Each row should say what it forces. The $17.7M row, for example, forces you to drop "back to plan" before you start.

Then split the overrun by cause. This is the move that separates a board-level answer. A single $6.6M number invites a single reaction, usually "cut AI". Three causes invite three decisions: scale the success, gate the slippage, stop the waste.

**The trap.** Opening with an apology for $6.6M, or opening with wins. Both answer a question the chair didn't ask.

**Rubric criteria earned:** overrun and 27.5 bp derived against the cushion; $17.7M spent shown; the cause split; savings netted before judging guidance.

### Stakeholders

**The question it answers:** who has a stake in this update, and how do you bring each along before the meeting?

**How to reason.** At board level most of the work happens before the room. The audit chair gets a pre-brief with the reconciliation, because a former CFO who sees a new number on the day will spend the slot on it. The CFO has to agree the 3.6 bp figure, since it's their guidance. The CEO wants AI seen as a strength, which is best served by leading with control. The COO needs to hear that the agent isn't being cut in peak. The CMO is the one who loses: marketing creative stops. Name that, and offer a test sized to give an answer next year.

Close with one idea: the board needs to trust the people running the spend more than any one number, so nobody hears a figure for the first time in the meeting.

**The trap.** A stakeholder table where nobody loses. Here the CMO does, and saying so is what makes the rest credible.

**Rubric criteria earned:** audit chair and pre-brief; CFO agreement first; the CMO's loss and offer; one idea to hold.

## 5. Pillar B: Decide

### Options

**The question it answers:** what could you ask the board for, and how does each framing fare?

**How to reason.** In a communication case, the options are framings and asks. Lay out four on one table: report only, cut to plan, re-base with actions, and ask for more. Score them on year-end spend, net hit against the cushion, and what the board learns about management.

Cut to plan fails on arithmetic. Only $0.3M of plan remains, and the agent's $2.0M of Q4 spend is the largest line. Stopping it during the holiday peak sends contacts back to the $7.00 contact center, which costs more than it saves. Report-only and ask-for-more leave the net hit at 12.5 bp, above the cushion. Re-base with actions is the only framing that lands inside 10 bp without touching the line that makes money.

**The trap.** Thinking "back to plan" is the responsible answer. Here it's the most expensive one.

**Rubric criteria earned:** at least three framings on one table; cut-to-plan shown to require stopping the agent; each scored against the cushion.

### Decision and Trade-offs

**The question it answers:** what do you recommend, what do you give up, and when would you revisit?

**How to reason.** Take decisions before you ask for anything. Pause marketing for Q4 and cut copilot seats to 2,500 before the meeting, so the board endorses actions instead of being asked to choose cuts. Then list what you give up: marketing's possible peak-season lift, 3,500 seats, a clean "on budget" story, and forecasting's $40M promise, which the evidence puts nearer $20M.

Say what is reversible. Both stops are: seats are monthly, and marketing can restart after a proper test. The one thing that isn't reversible is the board's trust if a second overrun follows. That's why the ask includes triggers: any line more than 10% over a quarter, or the agent's repeat-contact rate rising above the human baseline.

**The trap.** Asking the board what to cut. It reads as having no view, and it hands them a management decision.

**Rubric criteria earned:** one recommendation with pre-meeting actions; $22.475M derived; reversibility and trust; measurable triggers.

### Economics

**The question it answers:** what did each initiative cost and return, and what is the net effect on plan?

**How to reason.** Build one table: cost, measured value, net, per initiative. Be honest about the uneven ROI. The agent nets +$4.3M, content +$0.7M, forecasting −$5.9M this year with a real prize behind it, marketing about −$4.6M with a range that includes zero, and copilot −$3.6M, unmeasured. The total is $15.5M of value on $24.6M.

Then the number guidance depends on: net against the operating plan. After actions it's $4.475M over, less $3.6M of savings above plan, which is $0.875M, or 3.6 bp. Give the all-in figure too, because the chair will ask: $28.8M including the team, labelled as using an assumed $210k.

Finally the unit economics that change next year's budget: $4.13 a resolved contact against $7.00. Budget the agent per contact and its success can't read as an overrun again. Next year's proposal is $16.4M, with forecasting in two tranches.

**The trap.** Saying the programme "pays for itself". All-in, it doesn't yet. Two lines do.

**Rubric criteria earned:** cost and value per initiative with marketing's range; the 3.6 bp net; the all-in figure with its assumption labelled; the agent's unit economics.

## 6. Pillar C: Execute

### Org and Ownership

**The question it answers:** who owns spend and who owns value from now on?

**How to reason.** Ask why the overrun happened. Nobody owned value per initiative, and a variable cost was budgeted as a fixed sum. So the fix is structural: each initiative gets a business owner who signs its value number quarterly, FP&A reconciles it, and you keep ownership of spend and gates. Add an escalation rule: anything more than 10% over a quarterly line goes to the CFO before it's spent. The people on the marketing line move to the forecasting rollout.

**The trap.** A central approval board for every AI request. It slows the winners and measures value no better.

**Rubric criteria earned:** a value owner per initiative; one owner for spend and gates; escalation before spend.

### Plan and Milestones

**The question it answers:** what happens between now and the meeting, and over the next year?

**How to reason.** Nine days is a plan in itself. Days 1–3: decide the Q4 actions with the CFO and CEO, and get the CFO's signature on $22.475M. Days 4–5: pre-brief the audit chair. Day 9: the board. Then the year: a variable agent line from January, forecasting live in three more regions in Q1, and the Q2 board meeting deciding the $3.0M tranche against that gate.

**The trap.** Promising "a full review next quarter". It defers the decision and makes the overrun look open-ended.

**Rubric criteria earned:** actions and CFO sign-off before the board; the pre-brief; a dated, measurable tranche gate.

### Risk and Governance

**The question it answers:** what could go wrong next, and what controls it?

**How to reason.** The money already spent is sunk. The live risk is a second surprise. List the risks of the plan you're proposing: agent quality slipping at peak, agent usage outgrowing the unit budget, forecasting not repeating the pilot, marketing's next test being too small again, and the board hearing new numbers on the day. Each needs a control written into the budget or the process: a repeat-contact guard, a $4.50 unit ceiling, a held tranche, a test sized before it's funded, and a single reconciled data set.

**The trap.** Only talking about spend risk. You're scaling the agent, so the board needs to hear its quality guard.

**Rubric criteria earned:** a second surprise named as the main risk; a quality guard on the agent; triggers in the budget itself.

## 7. Pillar D: Land

### Metrics

**The question it answers:** how will the board know next year is working?

**How to reason.** One value metric and one guard per line. Agent-resolved contacts (about 1.8M at $7.5M and $4.13 each) against repeat-contact rate. Cost per resolved contact against customer satisfaction. Forecasting inventory reduction against stockouts. Spend against envelope against signed value figures. Active copilot seats against evidence of time saved. Report these quarterly to the audit committee.

**The trap.** Activity metrics as value: contacts handled, pages generated, seats provisioned. The board's question is money and risk.

**Rubric criteria earned:** paired guards; the agent measured per resolved contact; value signed and reconciled, not usage.

### Communication

**The question it answers:** what exactly do you say in the room?

**How to reason.** Write the opening line first, and put the bad number in it: "We'll spend $22.5M, $4.5M over what you approved. After the savings it produced, the net hit to operating profit is under $1M, about 4 basis points, so guidance holds." Then the one slide: five rows, spend vs plan, value, and the call for each. Nothing else on it.

Then rehearse the two hardest questions.

- **"Why trust next year's number?"** Don't promise to try harder. Point to structure: the biggest line is priced per result, and the rest is gated.
- **"Why not stop all of it?"** Use the cost of stopping. The agent's $7.5M next year avoids about $12.7M of contact-center fees, so stopping it costs money.

Set the cadence: quarterly to the audit committee from now on.

**The trap.** Leading with wins. The chair asked a control question, and answering a different one sounds like spin.

**Rubric criteria earned:** the overrun and net effect in the first sentence; a five-row slide; structural answers to both hard questions.

## 8. The decisions that separate strong answers

**Gross versus net.** Weak answers argue about $6.6M. Strong answers show that guidance depends on the net against the operating plan, which is 12.5 bp with no action and 3.6 bp after two cuts. That one reframe turns a defensive update into a controlled one.

**Acting before asking.** A board is there to oversee, not to pick cuts. Taking the marketing and seat decisions before the meeting, and reporting them as done, is the clearest signal of control you can give.

**Changing how the agent is budgeted.** Seeing that the agent's overrun is 0.5M extra wins, and moving it to a per-contact budget, fixes the cause rather than the symptom.

## 9. Common mistakes

- **Promising to get back to $18M.** $17.7M is already spent; the arithmetic doesn't allow it without stopping the agent at peak.
- **Cutting evenly.** A 20% cut across all five lines hurts the agent, which is the one making money.
- **Hiding the all-in number.** A former CFO will ask for it. Give $28.8M before they do.
- **Quoting marketing's +0.3% as a win.** Its range includes zero.
- **Keeping forecasting's $40M.** The evidence supports about $20M. Reset it now.
- **Talking models and vendors.** The board decides envelopes and gates, not tools.

## 10. Follow-ups an interviewer might ask

**The CFO asks you to leave out the all-in team cost. What do you do?**
Keep it, and agree the wording with the CFO first. The team is in the technology budget, so it's not part of the overrun, but the chair will ask, and an answer given only when asked looks evasive.

**A director says a competitor spends twice as much on AI. Should we?**
Not as a headline. Spend follows gates: the agent scales with resolved contacts automatically, and forecasting gets $3.0M more when three regions prove it. Matching someone else's spend is how the marketing line happened.

**What if Q1 shows the forecasting rollout doesn't repeat the pilot?**
The $3.0M tranche isn't released, the line stays at $2.0M, and you bring the board a smaller, honest case at Q2. The gate exists so that this outcome costs $2.0M, not $5.0M.

**The CMO goes to the CEO to save the marketing line. What's your answer?**
Offer a $0.4M test sized to detect the lift. A ±0.6 point range on a 0.3 point effect can't tell anyone anything. If the next test separates from zero, it comes back with evidence.

**What would make you ask for more money mid-year?**
The agent exceeding its forecast volume at or below $4.50 a contact. That's spend that brings $7.00 of savings with it, and the per-contact budget approves it automatically.

## 1. What the problem is really asking

On the surface this is a counteroffer: Maya has $520k on the table, you pay $360k, what do you offer? Underneath, it's two problems. One is retention: Maya has told you the main reason is 18 months of maintenance and on-call, and that she wants to lead the BEC work. The other is key-person risk: she is the only person who has run the refresh that keeps detection quality up, it's due in 10 weeks, and the last time it slipped the company lost $1.1M of ARR. That second problem exists whether she stays or goes.

Three facts in the brief carry the case:

1. **She told you the reason.** Money matters, but scope and on-call are what she named first. An answer that leads with cash is answering a different question.
2. **Your authority runs out at $400k.** The committee meets after her deadline, so a cash match can't honestly be offered. What you can control is the role, a raise to the band maximum, and a retention grant.
3. **The role she wants is the one you can't fill.** The BEC lead search has run 3 months with no finalist. Giving it to her solves a retention problem and a hiring problem at the same time, but it takes her off the refresh.

The interviewer is grading whether you listen before you offer, stay inside policy and say so plainly, and fix the refresh risk in either outcome.

## 2. The numbers that drive the decision

Work these out first.

**The gap.**
- Offer against current pay: $520k − $360k = **$160k**, a **44%** increase.
- Offer against the band maximum: $520k − $400k = **$120k** above what you can approve.
- A match would put her $520k − $385k = **$135k** above the next principal.

**What you can offer in 10 days.**
- Raise to the band maximum: $400k − $360k = **$40k** a year.
- Retention grant: $150k over 2 years = **$75k** a year.
- Year one: $400k + $75k = **$475k**, which is **$45k** below the offer. Say so.
- At $400k she is $15k above the $385k principal and $30k above the $370k one, within the band.

**Three-year cost.**
- Counter: $40k × 3 + $150k = **$270k**.
- Cash match: $160k × 3 = **$480k**, and it isn't in your authority.

**Funding.** The lead role was budgeted at $400k. Converted to a senior ML engineer at $300k, it frees **$100k a year**, **$300k** over 3 years, which covers the counter and adds someone to take Maya's maintenance work.

**The cost of losing her.**
- Agency fee: **$70k** (assumed); about 6 months to hire and 6 months to full speed.
- BEC: $3M / 12 = **$250k** of first-year ARR per month of delay. Another 6-month search for a lead puts the launch about 6 months later: 6 × $250k = **$1.5M** deferred, assuming the launch slips one month for each month without a lead.
- Refresh: the precedent is misses up 2.1× and **$1.1M** of ARR lost. That's history, not a forecast.

## 3. How to spend your time

Forty-five minutes. The trap here is spending them on the money.

| Minutes | What you do |
|---|---|
| 0–5 | Read the brief. Write down $160k, $120k, $475k vs $520k, $270k vs $480k, $250k a month. Circle "the main reason" and "only person who has run the refresh". |
| 5–12 | **Pillar A.** The two problems, and the stakeholder table with Maya's stated reason first. |
| 12–25 | **Pillar B.** Five options with "can you offer it in 10 days?" as a row; the role-first counter and its $45k gap; both sides of the economics. |
| 25–36 | **Pillar C.** Ownership after her role changes, the 10-day and 10-week plan, and the fairness controls. |
| 36–43 | **Pillar D.** Metrics beyond "she said yes", and what stays private. |
| 43–45 | Check that every pay figure is labelled as assumed. |

## 4. Pillar A: Frame

### Situation

**The question it answers:** what is really being decided, by when, and which numbers frame it?

**How to reason.** Rewrite "how much do we counter?" as "what can I offer within my authority in 10 days, and how do I make the refresh safe either way?" Put the numbers in a table: the $160k gap, $120k above the band, 10 days against 6 weeks, a refresh that only Maya has run, the $1.1M precedent, and a BEC lead search with no finalist. Then say what you'll do before offering anything: a stay conversation. Name the unknown, whether a role change without a cash match is enough, and say only she can answer it.

**The trap.** A bidding war. Matching cash answers a question she didn't ask.

**Rubric criteria earned:** the decision restated within authority and the deadline; the gaps derived; key-person risk named for both outcomes; the unknown named.

### Stakeholders

**The question it answers:** who has a stake, and how do you bring them along?

**How to reason.** Maya first, with her reason in her words: scope and on-call, then pay. Then your manager (quality, BEC, no exception), HR (a defensible offer), Ravi (who takes the refresh and deserves credit for it), the on-call rotation (which loses a person), and the two other principals, who will notice if a colleague at the same level jumps ahead. Add BEC sales and product, who get a named lead.

**The trap.** Forgetting the other principals. Retention offers are noticed, and the next equity complaint starts here.

**Rubric criteria earned:** Maya's reason first; HR, manager, Ravi and on-call; the other principals; one idea to hold.

## 5. Pillar B: Decide

### Options

**The question it answers:** what are the real options?

**How to reason.** Five options on one table: let her go well, match $520k, a role-first counter, promise future pay or promotion, and cash and equity with no role change. Add the row that decides most of them: **can you offer it in 10 days?** A match can't be offered, because the committee meets in 6 weeks. A promise of future pay or promotion isn't yours to make. Cash without a role change pays her to keep doing what she wants to stop. Letting her go is a real option, and it needs its own plan.

**The trap.** Opening with a number. The conversation then becomes about the $160k gap, not the work.

**Rubric criteria earned:** letting her go included; the match ruled out on deadline and band; promises rejected; one comparable table.

### Decision and Trade-offs

**The question it answers:** what do you offer, what do you give up, and what changes it?

**How to reason.** Offer the role first: BEC lead now, off on-call. Then the money within policy: $400k and the $150k grant, which is $475k in year one, $45k short. Say the gap out loud. Give-ups: Maya as the refresh runner (Ravi takes it, with Maya reviewing), the external lead search, a cash match, and some pay compression with the other principals. The role is reversible; the raise and grant aren't. Revisit at 6 months: BEC more than a month behind, Maya back on maintenance more than a day a week, or the refresh needing more than her review.

**The trap.** Overselling. "We'll get you to $520k" breaks trust if the committee says no.

**Rubric criteria earned:** role first; the $45k gap said plainly; give-ups named; dated triggers.

### Economics

**The question it answers:** what does keeping her cost, and what does losing her cost?

**How to reason.** Put both sides over 3 years. The counter is $270k; a match would be $480k. Losing her costs a $70k fee and a year before a replacement is at full speed, about $1.5M of deferred BEC ARR at $250k a month, and a refresh run on a half-finished runbook, with $1.1M as the precedent rather than a forecast. Then show the funding move: converting the lead role to a senior hire frees $300k over 3 years, about the cost of the counter. Label every pay figure as assumed. Reveal the model answer for the full table.

**The trap.** Comparing her pay with a replacement's pay. The cost of losing her is the BEC launch and the refresh.

**Rubric criteria earned:** counter and match over 3 years; the cost of loss priced; the converted role funds it; assumptions labelled.

## 6. Pillar C: Execute

### Org and Ownership

**The question it answers:** who owns what after her role changes?

**How to reason.** Name an owner for everything she held. Maya owns BEC, with the month-9 launch. Ravi owns the refresh: Maya reviews this one, and Ravi runs the next alone. A senior engineer joins on-call so the rotation stays at 4. The converted senior hire takes her maintenance work. You and HR own the pay and the grant.

**The trap.** Leaving Maya on the refresh "for one more quarter". That's the work she wants to stop, and the risk stays.

**Rubric criteria earned:** Ravi owns the refresh with Maya reviewing; on-call kept at 4; a BEC remit with a milestone.

### Plan and Milestones

**The question it answers:** what happens in the next 10 days and the next 10 weeks?

**How to reason.** Days 1–2: the stay conversation, then brief your manager and HR. Days 3–5: HR approves the raise and grant, and the BEC role goes to Maya in writing. Day 10: she decides. In parallel, and regardless of her answer: Ravi leads refresh prep from week 2, the runbook is finished and a dry run done by week 6 (the gate: Ravi completes it with Maya only reviewing), and the refresh ships at week 10. Revisit at month 6.

**The trap.** Waiting for her answer before starting the refresh plan.

**Rubric criteria earned:** stay conversation first; HR approval inside the window; runbook and dry run before the refresh; the 6-month revisit.

### Risk and Governance

**The question it answers:** what could go wrong with fairness, policy and the refresh?

**How to reason.** Offer only what HR has approved, and say the committee isn't involved. Tell HR that $400k puts Maya $15k and $30k above the other principals, give the scope reason, and ask for principal pay to be reviewed at the next cycle; her offer is market data for the whole band. Admit that the BEC role is something you should have offered earlier, and start stay conversations with your other senior engineers, so the lesson isn't "get an outside offer". If she declines, the refresh plan is already running, she spends her notice on the runbook, and the converted role is reopened as the lead search.

**The trap.** Rewarding the outside offer and ignoring everyone else.

**Rubric criteria earned:** offer within policy; equity flagged to HR; the incentive problem addressed; a plan if she leaves.

## 7. Pillar D: Land

### Metrics

**The question it answers:** how do you know it worked?

**How to reason.** "She stayed" is the start, not the result. Track BEC against the month-9 launch, with her time on maintenance and on-call (target zero) as the guard. Track the refresh shipping at week 10, with customer-reported misses as the guard. Track Ravi running the next refresh alone, with his hours as the guard. Track on-call at 4 people, with pages per person as the guard.

**The trap.** Declaring success the day she accepts. The usual failure is a counter that buys a year and changes nothing.

**Rubric criteria earned:** paired guards; refresh with misses as guard; on-call load; BEC milestones.

### Communication

**The question it answers:** what does each audience hear, and what stays private?

**How to reason.** Maya hears the role, the numbers and the honest gap. Your manager hears both outcomes priced: $270k to keep her, funded by the converted role, against $1.5M of BEC delay and the refresh risk. HR hears that it's within policy and gets the request for a principal pay review. Ravi hears that he owns the refresh. The team, after she decides, hears about a role change, never about an outside offer. If she stays, check in at 30, 90 and 180 days.

**The trap.** Explaining to the team why Maya got BEC.

**Rubric criteria earned:** four or more audiences; the offer kept private; both outcomes priced for your manager; a follow-up cadence.

## 8. The decisions that separate strong answers

**Listening before offering.** Weak answers open with a number. Strong answers hold the stay conversation first and lead with the role she asked for.

**Fixing the risk in both outcomes.** The refresh plan (Ravi, the runbook, a dry run by week 6) is the same whether she stays or leaves. Strong answers start it on day 1.

**Staying inside your authority, out loud.** Saying "this doesn't match $520k, and I won't promise the committee" builds the trust that a counter needs.

## 9. Common mistakes

- **Matching the cash.** Not in your authority, $135k above the next principal, and it doesn't address her reason.
- **Promising a promotion or a committee outcome.** If it doesn't happen, she leaves angrier.
- **Leaving her on the refresh.** The work she wants to stop, and the risk stays.
- **Ignoring the other principals.** They're the next retention problem.
- **Telling the team about the offer.** It teaches everyone how to get a raise.
- **No plan if she says no.** The refresh is due in 10 weeks either way.

## 10. Follow-ups an interviewer might ask

**She says the role is great but she needs $520k.**
Thank her and be straight: you can't promise above $400k before her deadline. You can take the principal band to the committee in 6 weeks with her offer as market data, with no promise of the outcome. If $520k is the floor, let her go well, and spend the notice period on the runbook.

**She accepts and asks for two engineers on BEC.**
The converted senior role is one. A second would come from the BEC plan's own headcount, or from moving one of the 11 once the refresh no longer depends on Maya.

**Ravi says he doesn't want to own the refresh.**
Ask why. If it's load, take other work off him and make the dry run the gate. If it's interest, find another senior, but don't hand it back to Maya.

**A month later, another senior engineer brings you an outside offer.**
Hold a stay conversation the same way: reason first, offer within policy. Then ask why you didn't hold it before the offer, and schedule stay conversations with the rest of the team.

**What if she'd just said "it's the money"?**
Then the counter is $400k and the grant, $475k, and an honest statement that it doesn't match. You'd still move the refresh to Ravi, because the key-person risk doesn't depend on her reason.

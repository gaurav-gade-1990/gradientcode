## 1. What the problem is really asking

On the surface this is "what do you do about an underperformer?" Underneath, it's two separate problems that most candidates merge into one. The first is a delivery problem: the go-live gate depends on one person, and at that person's recent pace it finishes eight weeks late. The second is a people problem: a senior engineer with two strong years behind them has struggled for six weeks, hasn't had clear written feedback, and may be dealing with a parent's serious illness.

If you solve both with one move, you get one of two bad answers. "Start a PIP" uses the people process to fix a delivery problem: it's unfair, HR policy blocks it, and it still leaves the harness in Dana's hands. "Give Dana space and cover quietly" is kind but loses the customer and lets the team carry the cost.

Three facts in the brief carry the case:

1. **The harness is the critical path, and it's single-threaded.** At Dana's recent rate it lands in about week 14, which puts go-live more than 4 weeks late and gives the $4.8M chain the right to leave.
2. **No feedback is on record.** One verbal mention isn't documented feedback, so a formal step isn't available yet, and wouldn't be fair if it were.
3. **There's a likely cause you mustn't diagnose.** The parent's illness may matter. Your job is to offer support through HR, not to ask for details or assume.

The interviewer is grading whether you can protect the project and the person at the same time, with a plan that works whatever Dana's situation turns out to be.

## 2. The numbers that drive the decision

Work these out first.

**Dana's recent rate.** 4 of 11 tickets = **36%** of plan.

**The critical path.**
- At full pace Dana does 5 days × 60% = **3 engineer-days a week** on the harness, so 15 days takes 5 weeks.
- At 36%: 3 × 4/11 = **1.1 a week**, so 15 / 1.1 ≈ **13.75 weeks**.
- The plan needs 4 weeks after the harness (tuning in weeks 7–9, go-live in week 10), so go-live lands at about week 17.75: **7.75 weeks late**.
- Credits: a part week counts, so 8 × $40k = **$320k**, and more than 4 weeks late triggers the **$4.8M** termination right.

**The team's capacity.**
- 6 engineers × 50 days × 60% = **180** engineer-days, minus 20 committed elsewhere = **160**, against 150 needed: **10 days of slack**.
- Dana's planned 30 days at 36% is 10.9, a loss of **19.1**, so the team is **9.1 days short** overall.

**The contractor.**
- 6 weeks × 40 hours × $150 = **$36k**, inside the $40k budget.
- 6 weeks × 5 days × 80% = 24 days, minus 3 to onboard = **21 engineer-days**.
- With the contractor, even if Dana stays at 36%: 160 − 19.1 + 21 = **161.9**, so **11.9 days of slack**.

**An exit.** Backfill $230k / 2 = **$115k** and 4 months to hire, after go-live. Five engineers give 5 × 30 − 20 = 130, or 151 with the contractor: no slack.

**The hidden cost.** Two peers at 3 hours a week for 10 weeks = **60 hours**.

## 3. How to spend your time

Forty-five minutes. Pillar C carries more weight here than in most cases, because the fair process is the plan.

| Minutes | What you do |
|---|---|
| 0–5 | Read the brief. Write down week 14, 7.75 weeks late, $320k, 10 days of slack, $36k for 21 days. Circle "nothing is in writing". |
| 5–12 | **Pillar A.** Two problems, the frame table, and Dana first in the stakeholder table. |
| 12–24 | **Pillar B.** Four options, the recommendation with a week-4 checkpoint, and the costs. |
| 24–36 | **Pillar C.** Owners, the two-track plan, and the fairness controls with HR. |
| 36–43 | **Pillar D.** Metrics including the support you promised, and what stays private. |
| 43–45 | Check that every number is derived and every judgment is about work, not character. |

## 4. Pillar A: Frame

### Situation

**The question it answers:** what is the real problem, and which numbers frame it?

**How to reason.** Name the two problems separately. Then put the numbers in a table: 36% of plan, the harness at week 14, go-live 7.75 weeks late, 10 days of slack, a 9.1-day shortfall and 6 hours a week of hidden peer review. Write down what's known (dated facts) and what isn't (the cause), and say you won't guess at it. Out of scope: Dana's long-term future, which is decided later by a process.

**The trap.** One problem, one fix. Either the project or Dana loses.

**Rubric criteria earned:** two problems separated; the harness at week 14 derived; slack and shortfall derived; the unknown cause left unknown.

### Stakeholders

**The question it answers:** who has a stake, and how do you bring them along?

**How to reason.** Put Dana first. Dana wants fairness, privacy and room to recover, and has a right to clear expectations before being judged against them. Then the HR partner (a consistent, documented process), your manager (the go-live), Lee (a fair workload and credit), the two reviewing peers (their time back), the chain (week 10 at 97%) and the team (a plan they believe). Say what Lee takes on and what comes off Lee's plate, since the re-plan moves work onto someone who didn't ask for it.

**The trap.** Leaving Dana out of the stakeholder table, as if they were the problem rather than a person with a stake.

**Rubric criteria earned:** Dana first with what Dana needs; HR, manager, chain and peers; Lee's workload; one idea to hold.

## 5. Pillar B: Decide

### Options

**The question it answers:** what are the real options, on the go-live and on fairness?

**How to reason.** Four options on one table: wait and cover quietly, a formal PIP now with Dana keeping the harness, talk-expectations-support-re-plan, and removing Dana now. Score each on harness date, go-live, fairness and HR process. Waiting and the PIP both leave the harness at week 14. The PIP also fails HR policy, because nothing is documented. Removing Dana is unfair, carries legal exposure, and throws away the person who knows the harness. Only the re-plan with support passes both tests.

**The trap.** Starting with the PIP. Without prior feedback it reads as a paper trail for an exit.

**Rubric criteria earned:** waiting included; the PIP blocked; the removal shown unfair and costly; one comparable table.

### Decision and Trade-offs

**The question it answers:** what do you do, what do you give up, and what changes it?

**How to reason.** Make one call with two halves: this week, a private conversation, written expectations and support; and a re-plan that makes Lee the harness owner with Dana on a defined slice, and a contractor on Lee's price-matching work. List the give-ups: Dana's sole ownership, Lee's planned work, $36k of the budget, and a quick resolution. The re-plan is reversible, since ownership can come back after go-live. A formal process isn't easily reversed, so it waits for the week-4 checkpoint and its written criteria: met, not met, or new context such as leave.

**The trap.** Moving the harness silently. Without the conversation first, it feels like a demotion that came before the feedback.

**Rubric criteria earned:** one call covering plan and process; give-ups named; reversibility; the week-4 checkpoint.

### Economics

**The question it answers:** what does each option cost?

**How to reason.** Use the same rows for all four: credits, revenue at risk, direct spend and hidden cost. Waiting costs about $320k of credits and puts $4.8M a year at risk, plus 60 hours of peer time. The re-plan costs $36k for 21 engineer-days, which covers the 9.1-day shortfall twice. An exit costs $115k and a 4-month vacancy that lands after go-live, and leaves 151 days against 150 with no slack. Label the assumptions: the contractor rate, 80% and 60% focus, and the backfill cost and time.

**The trap.** Seeing the exit as the fast, cheap fix. It's neither.

**Rubric criteria earned:** waiting priced; the contractor priced from hours and rate; the exit priced; assumptions labelled.

## 6. Pillar C: Execute

### Org and Ownership

**The question it answers:** who owns what for the next 10 weeks?

**How to reason.** Give the harness to Lee, who has run it. Give Dana a defined, visible slice of it (the test-set loader and scoring) with dated tasks, scoped so a missed date doesn't move go-live. The contractor gets bounded, non-critical work: the price-matching rules. You own Dana's expectations and check-ins, with HR as partner, and you own customer status.

**The trap.** Taking Dana off everything. It's a verdict before feedback, and it wastes the person who knows the harness best.

**Rubric criteria earned:** a harness owner who isn't Dana, with Dana on a defined part; bounded contractor work; you own the process with HR.

### Plan and Milestones

**The question it answers:** what happens week by week?

**How to reason.** Run two tracks side by side. On the project track: re-plan in week 0, Lee and the contractor start in week 1, Gate 1 at week 6 (the harness scores the full 2,000-menu-page set), Gate 2 at week 9 (97% field accuracy), go-live in week 10. On Dana's track: HR briefed and the conversation in week 0, written expectations within two days, weekly check-ins, written check-ins at weeks 2 and 4, and the week-4 checkpoint decided with HR. Order matters: Dana hears about the re-plan first, from you, before standup.

**The trap.** Announcing the re-plan to the team before talking to Dana.

**Rubric criteria earned:** conversation in week 0 before the re-plan; harness by week 6; written check-ins; a measurable gate.

### Risk and Governance

**The question it answers:** what keeps this fair, documented and lawful?

**How to reason.** Four habits. Write expectations as observable outcomes with dates: named tasks, PRs passing CI before merge, notice before missing meetings. Hold Dana to the same bar as other L5s, and have HR check it. Offer real support through HR, such as the EAP, flexible hours and leave, without asking for medical details. Keep the record two-sided: Dana can respond in writing, and the notes show the feedback, support and time given. If Dana takes family leave, it goes through HR and has no effect on assessment, and the plan already works without Dana.

**The trap.** Documenting only the misses. A fair record shows what you did as a manager too.

**Rubric criteria earned:** observable, dated expectations; support without medical questions; HR in before the conversation; no retaliation for leave.

## 7. Pillar D: Land

### Metrics

**The question it answers:** how do you know it's working?

**How to reason.** Pair each metric with a guard. Harness by week 6 is guarded by Lee's after-hours work. 97% accuracy is guarded by checking that no scope was quietly cut to reach it. Dana meeting expectations is guarded by whether the check-ins and support you promised actually happened. Peer review workload falling from 6 hours a week is guarded by review quality on the critical path.

**The trap.** Tracking only Dana's output. If the check-ins didn't happen, the process failed.

**Rubric criteria earned:** paired guards; the harness and the 97% gate; peer and after-hours workload; support tracked.

### Communication

**The question it answers:** what does each audience hear, and what stays private?

**How to reason.** Dana hears the facts, the expectations in writing, the support available and the check-in cadence. HR hears the facts and the draft before the conversation. Your manager hears the risk in numbers (week 14 heading to week 6 for $36k). Lee hears that they own the gate and what comes off their plate. The team hears that the critical path was re-planned so nobody carries it alone. The chain hears milestones. Nobody beyond Dana, HR and your manager hears anything about Dana.

**The trap.** Explaining the re-plan to the team by talking about Dana.

**Rubric criteria earned:** four or more audiences; Dana's situation kept private; your manager told in numbers; a cadence.

## 8. The decisions that separate strong answers

**Separating the plan from the process.** Weak answers use one lever for both. Strong answers fix the go-live with ownership and a contractor, and handle Dana with feedback and support, so neither depends on the other.

**Finding the critical path, not the average.** The team-wide shortfall is only 9.1 days, which looks manageable. The single-threaded harness at week 14 is what makes the case urgent.

**Fairness as something you can check.** Strong answers write expectations as dated outcomes, track the support they promised, and bring HR in first.

## 9. Common mistakes

- **Jumping to a PIP.** Policy blocks it, and it reads as pre-deciding the outcome.
- **Diagnosing the cause.** Asking about the parent's health, or assuming it explains everything.
- **Covering quietly.** It feels kind, but it costs the peers 60 hours and the chain its go-live.
- **Exiting fast.** $115k, 4 months, and the harness knowledge walks out.
- **Telling the team.** Privacy is part of dignity and part of a defensible record.
- **Vague expectations.** "Be more engaged" can't be met or measured.

## 10. Follow-ups an interviewer might ask

**Dana says the parent's illness is the reason, and asks for leave.**
Thank them for telling you and connect them with HR straight away for family leave and the EAP. Pause the performance expectations while they're on leave. The re-plan already covers the harness, so the project doesn't depend on the answer.

**Dana meets the week-4 expectations. What now?**
Say so, in writing. Keep the weekly check-ins for another month, and return scope gradually after go-live, including harness ownership if Dana wants it.

**Dana doesn't meet them, with no new context.**
With HR, start the formal process: a written plan of at least 45 days, with clear outcomes and support. The project is already protected, so the process can take the time it needs.

**Lee pushes back on taking the harness.**
Acknowledge the extra workload, take the price-matching rules off Lee's plate (that's what the contractor is for), and make Lee the named owner of the go-live gate, which is visible, promotable work.

**Your manager says "just replace Dana".**
Show the numbers: $115k, a 4-month vacancy after go-live, 151 days against 150 with no slack, and no documented basis. The re-plan costs $36k and lands the harness at week 6.
